# How sound travels

## Making sound

Every sound starts with something that vibrates, which means it moves back and forth very quickly. When you pluck a guitar string, it vibrates. When the string stops vibrating, the sound stops. Your voice works the same way: two small folds in your throat, the vocal cords, vibrate when air passes them.

## How sound travels

A vibrating object pushes on the particles next to it. Those particles bump into their neighbours, and the vibration is passed along like a push through a line of people. This is how sound travels from the guitar to your ear. Sound needs particles to travel through: it can move through air, water and solids.

## Space is silent

Space is almost empty; there are almost no particles in it. With nothing to pass the vibration along, sound cannot travel through space. Astronauts outside their spacecraft talk by radio, which uses radio waves that can cross empty space.

## Speed of sound

Sound travels at about 340 metres per second in air, about 1,500 metres per second in water and more than 5,000 metres per second in steel. In solids the particles are packed closely together, so each one passes the vibration to the next very quickly. Light is much faster than sound, which is why you see lightning before you hear the thunder.

## Protecting your ears

Very loud sounds, such as a jet engine nearby or music played loudly through headphones for a long time, can damage the tiny hair cells inside the ear. Damaged hair cells do not grow back, so ear protection matters.
